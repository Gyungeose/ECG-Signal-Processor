## The Heartbeat as Electricity

Open a cardiac physiology textbook and you'll see two parallel narratives: *mechanical* (atria contract, blood moves into ventricles, ventricles contract, blood leaves the heart) and *electrical* (SA node fires, depolarization spreads through atria, reaches AV node, spreads through ventricles, repolarizes). The ECG is entirely the *electrical* story.

The mechanical story lags behind the electrical one. **Electrical depolarization comes first. Mechanical contraction follows, delayed by the time it takes for calcium to flood the sarcoplasmic reticulum and actin-myosin to cross-bridge.** If you see normal electrical activity but no mechanical squeeze, you have a problem (that's called pulseless electrical activity, or PEA, and it kills people quickly).

The normal ECG is a map of this electrical sequence. **Each waveform — each peak, each valley — corresponds to depolarization or repolarization of a specific cardiac chamber, happening at a specific time.**

---

## The P Wave: Atrial Depolarization

**What's happening:** The SA node (right atrium, near the IVC) fires. Depolarization spreads through atrial muscle, right to left and superior to inferior (mostly).

**On the ECG:** A small, rounded bump before the QRS. Normal duration <120 ms. Normal amplitude <2.5 mm (0.25 mV) in limb leads.

**Direction:** In lead II (looking at the heart from the left leg), normal atrial depolarization moves *toward* you, so the P wave is usually **upright**. In lead aVR (looking from the right shoulder), it moves *away*, so P is **inverted**.

> [!tip]- Retrieval Cue
> If you see an inverted P wave in lead II (where it should be upright), what has changed about the origin of the depolarization?

**Morphology clue:** A **biphasic P wave** (part up, part down) in lead V1 suggests depolarization that's partly moving toward and partly away from that lead — classic for normal sinus rhythm, but can also flag atrial enlargement (the second half of the P wave represents the enlarged left atrium catching up).

---

## The PR Interval: AV Node Delay

**What's happening:** Depolarization reached the AV node. The AV node is *slow* — it's the bottleneck of the conduction system. Depolarization crawls through it (0.05–0.1 seconds), then races down the bundle of His to the ventricles.

**On the ECG:** The flat line between the end of the P wave and the start of the QRS. Duration **120–200 ms** (0.12–0.2 seconds) is normal.

**Why is it flat?** Because the entire depolarization is happening *inside* the AV node, buried in the middle of the heart where no surface electrode can see it. It's electrically silent to the outside observer.

**What prolongs it?** Anything that slows AV conduction:
- Increased parasympathetic tone (vagal stimulation, sleep)
- Beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers
- AV nodal disease
- Age (PR interval creeps longer as you get older)

**What shortens it?** 
- Increased sympathetic tone
- Accessory pathways (the depolarization bypasses the AV node's delay)

> [!example]- Worked Example
> **12-year-old soccer player:** ECG shows PR interval of 0.14 seconds. Parents are told it's normal. You measure it at home and confirm: consistently 0.14. This is *within* the normal range (120–200 ms) but on the *short* end. Explanation: high resting parasympathetic tone (athletic conditioning increases vagal tone). This is normal variation. Contrast with a 50-year-old with PR 0.24 (prolonged, needs workup).

## The QRS Complex: Ventricular Depolarization

**What's happening:** The bundle of His splits into left and right bundle branches. Depolarization spreads through the septum first (right to left), then through the main ventricular muscle (endocardium outward).

**On the ECG:** The **largest and most distinctive deflection** on the strip. Duration **<120 ms** (< 0.12 seconds) is normal.

The QRS is actually three waves rolled into one name:
- **Q wave:** Initial small negative deflection (rare; represents septal depolarization moving left-to-right)
- **R wave:** The tall positive deflection (main ventricular mass depolarizing)
- **S wave:** The negative deflection after the R (later ventricular depolarization moving toward the baseline)

Not every QRS has all three. You might see just RS, or just QR, depending on the lead orientation.

**Amplitude:** Normally tall — anywhere from 5 mm to 20+ mm depending on the lead and patient size. **>25 mm in limb leads or >35 mm in precordial leads suggests left ventricular hypertrophy** (the muscle is thicker, so the electrical signal is bigger).

**Duration:** 
- **<120 ms (narrow complex):** Depolarization is traveling down the normal His-Purkinje system. Conduction is fast.
- **≥120 ms (wide complex):** Conduction is slow. Either one of the bundle branches is blocked (the ventricles depolarize sequentially instead of in parallel), or the rhythm is originating in the ventricles instead of the atria (ectopic rhythm bypasses the fast His-Purkinje system).

```
Normal conduction (narrow QRS):
        ┌─ Node ─┐
        │        │
    RBB         LBB
     │            │
  Right V      Left V   → Both depolarize together → fast QRS

Blocked RBB (wide QRS):
        ┌─ Node ─┐
        │        X
       RBB       LBB
        │         │
   Waits for L → depolarizes slowly   → wide QRS
```

> Why is a QRS that originates in the ventricle (rather than coming down from the SA node) necessarily wide? Draw or explain the conduction pathway.

## The ST Segment: Early Repolarization

**What's happening:** Ventricles are *fully* depolarized. The entire ventricular mass is positive inside. There is no voltage difference between any two regions of ventricular tissue — they're all at the same potential.

**On the ECG:** A flat line between the end of the QRS and the start of the T wave. **Normally isoelectric** (at baseline, zero voltage). Duration varies with heart rate.

**Why is it there?** The repolarization hasn't started yet. The ventricles are in the **plateau phase** of the action potential — calcium channels are still open, keeping the membrane potential positive. No net current flow = no voltage = flat line.

**Clinical relevance:** 
- **ST elevation:** The plateau phase is being interrupted by something. ischemic injury (MI) or inflammation (pericarditis) can displace the baseline voltage. This is **urgent**.
- **ST depression:** The ST segment is *below* baseline. Indicates **subendocardial ischemia** — the inside of the ventricle is being starved of blood while the outside is fine. This is ischemia-in-progress.

> [!tip]- Retrieval Cue
> What has to be physiologically true about the ventricles during the ST segment for it to be flat?

## The T Wave: Ventricular Repolarization

**What's happening:** Ventricular repolarization begins. Potassium channels open, calcium channels close. The membrane potential swings from positive to negative. The wavefront of repolarization spreads through the ventricles (typically *opposite* to the direction of depolarization — this is important).

**On the ECG:** A broader, gentler hill compared to the QRS. Normal duration is part of the **QT interval** (covered next). Normal morphology: **usually upright in the same leads where the QRS is upright**.

**Why usually?** Because repolarization travels mostly from epicardium (outside) to endocardium (inside), which is *opposite* the direction of depolarization (which goes endo → epi). This reversal means the T wave and QRS usually point the same way in each lead — a principle called **ventricular repolarization concordance**.

**Exception:** Leads looking at the ventricle *head-on* (like V1, looking at the right ventricle end-on) might show an *inverted* T wave in young healthy people. This is normal variation.

**Clinical red flag:** **T wave inversion** in a lead where it should be upright (e.g., inverted in V2–V4, the anterior leads) suggests recent ischemia. **Hyperacute T waves** (extremely tall, peaked) suggest hyperkalemia.

---

## The QT Interval: Total Ventricular Electrical Activity

**What's measured:** From the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave. Represents the entire time the ventricles are depolarized *and* repolarizing.

**Normal duration:** **<440 ms** in men, **<450 ms** in women (sex difference because of testosterone slowing repolarization). But this is *rate-dependent* — faster heart rate = shorter QT. Slower rate = longer QT.

**Rate correction:** Clinicians use **QTc (corrected QT)** via Bazett's formula:

$$\text{QTc} = \frac{\text{QT}}{\sqrt{\text{RR interval}}}$$

This normalizes the QT to a standard rate. **QTc >500 ms is abnormal** and carries risk of **torsades de pointes** — a polymorphic ventricular arrhythmia that can degenerate to V-fib.

**What prolongs QT?**
- Drugs (antiarrhythmics like sotalol, quinidine; antipsychotics like haloperidol; antibiotics like azithromycin)
- Electrolyte disorders (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia — K, Mg, Ca all regulate repolarization)
- Congenital long QT syndrome (ion channel mutations)
- Bradycardia (slower heart rate = more time to repolarize)

**What shortens QT?**
- Hyperkalemia
- Hypercalcemia

> [!example]- Worked Example
> **65-year-old man on sotalol (antiarrhythmic) for AFib:** ECG shows QTc 520 ms. He's prescribed azithromycin for pneumonia. Four days later, he has a syncopal episode (faints). ECG shows polymorphic VT (torsades). Explanation: sotalol *already* prolonging QT. Azithromycin *further* prolongs it. QTc goes from 520 to ~580 ms. Repolarization is so prolonged and so late that late-phase afterdepolarizations (abnormal depolarizations during repolarization) trigger arrhythmias. The combination was dangerous; each drug alone might have been fine.

## Summary

The ECG waveform is a map of electrical events:
- **P wave** = atrial depolarization
- **PR interval** = AV node delay (electrically silent)
- **QRS** = ventricular depolarization (narrow = normal pathways; wide = abnormal)
- **ST segment** = plateau phase (normally flat; elevation or depression = ischemia)
- **T wave** = ventricular repolarization
- **QT interval** = depolarization + repolarization time (rate-corrected to QTc; prolongation = risk)

Each waveform has normal ranges. Deviations signal pathology — but interpretation requires understanding *why* each component exists in the first place.
