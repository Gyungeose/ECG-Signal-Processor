## The First Confusion

Walk into a cardiology ward and grab the nearest ECG machine. You'll see a roll of paper printing what looks like a seismic readout — peaks and troughs marching across graph paper, numbers in the corner, the machine humming like it's decoding the heart itself.

Here's what nobody tells you: **the ECG does not measure contraction. It measures electrical activity.** Two fundamentally different things. You can have electrical activity with no mechanical squeeze. You can have a mechanical squeeze with no electrical signal — for about four seconds before the heart fibrillates and consciousness leaves. The machine sees voltage. The heart does work.

This distinction is not semantic. It is *clinical*. An abnormal ECG might mean the heart is failing. Or it might mean the patient is having a panic attack, or they've swallowed a bottle of antiarrhythmics, or their chest is bandaged too tight and the leads can't sense anything. **The ECG is not the heart. It is a recording of what happens *before* the heart contracts.**

---

## What the ECG Actually Measures

The ECG measures **voltage differences** across the surface of the body — specifically, the voltage difference between two points on the skin as the wave of electrical depolarization spreads through the cardiac chambers.

When a cardiac myocyte depolarizes, ions move across the cell membrane. The inside becomes positive relative to the outside (momentarily). This charge separation creates a dipole — a negative pole behind the wavefront, a positive pole ahead of it. As the wavefront propagates through tissue, the dipole moves through space.

An electrode *at the surface* detects the voltage generated by this moving dipole. Two electrodes detect the *difference* in voltage. This voltage, plotted against time, is the ECG tracing.

> [!tip]- Retrieval Cue
> Why does putting an electrode on the chest measure something happening inside the heart? What physical principle connects a moving charge inside the thorax to a voltage difference you can measure with a wire?

The math is Coulomb's law wrapped in tissue conductivity. The physiology is this: **every moving depolarization wavefront broadcasts its electrical signature to the surrounding medium.** The body is conductive (it's mostly saltwater). Electrodes are just antennas.

## The 12-Lead System: Same Heart, Different Viewpoints

A 12-lead ECG is not 12 different heartbeats. It is **12 simultaneous snapshots of the same heart from 12 different anatomical vantage points.** Think of it like a cube:

```
          aVR
            |
            |
----- I ---- • ---- -aVL
            |
            |
           II
           
(aVF, V1-V6 in other planes)
```

You need multiple viewpoints because the heart is a three-dimensional organ. A depolarization wavefront moving *toward* electrode A looks positive. The *same wavefront* moving *away from* electrode B looks negative. If you only record one lead, you're blind to two-thirds of what's happening.

The 12 leads fall into two families:

### **Limb Leads (6)**
- **Standard leads:** I, II, III — record voltage differences between the left arm, right arm, and left leg. These are spaced 120° apart around the body.
- **Augmented leads:** aVR, aVL, aVF — record voltage between a limb and the "average" of the other two. These fill in the 60° gaps.

Collectively, they give you the **frontal plane** — what the heart looks like if you're standing in front of the patient.

### **Precordial Leads (6)**
- V1–V6 are electrodes placed in a line across the chest, from right sternal border (V1) to the left midaxillary line (V6).

They give you the **horizontal (transverse) plane** — what the heart looks like if you're cutting the patient in half at nipple level and looking up.

Together: frontal plane + transverse plane = a crude 3D reconstruction.

> [!example]- Worked Example
> **Scenario:** You see a large positive QRS in lead II and a large negative QRS in lead aVR.
> 
> Why? The depolarization wavefront is moving *toward* the left leg (II positive) and *away from* the right shoulder (aVR negative). This tells you the wavefront is heading inferolaterally — your atrial depolarization or your ventricular depolarization is spreading from the right upper heart toward the left lower heart. This is *normal* for atrial depolarization (P wave). It would be *abnormal* for ventricular depolarization (suggests an ectopic rhythm originating high in the ventricles).

---

## Limb Leads vs Precordial Leads: Territory

| **Lead Group** | **Anatomical View** | **Territory** | **Key Distinction** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limb leads | Frontal plane | Rate, rhythm, axis, inferior/lateral pathology | You see left-right and superior-inferior motion |
| Precordial leads | Transverse plane | Anterior-posterior and right-left motion; septal, anterior, lateral pathology | You see depth — how deeply the wavefront penetrates the ventricles |

Why this matters: a **small myocardial infarction** in the posterior wall might be *invisible* in limb leads but *obvious* in the precordial leads — because the infarcted tissue is posterior (behind the left ventricle), and the precordial leads are the only ones looking directly at that region.

## Single-Lead vs 12-Lead: The Clinic vs The ED

A **single-lead recording** (like those continuous-monitoring strips in MIT-BIH) tells you:
- Rate and rhythm
- QRS duration (is it wide or narrow?)
- Crude axis (roughly, which way is the QRS pointing?)

It *cannot* tell you:
- Whether an MI is anterior, inferior, or lateral
- Whether axis deviation is pathological or just anatomical variation
- Whether a wide complex is a bundle branch block or an ectopic rhythm (both can look similar in a single lead)

A **12-lead ECG** lets you:
- Localize pathology to a specific region
- Assess conduction (axis, intervals, blocks)
- Distinguish morphologies that look identical in one lead but different in another

**Clinical reality:** In the ED, you always want 12 leads when possible. On a telemetry monitor in the ward, single-lead is fine for rate/rhythm trending. But if rhythm changes, grab a 12-lead before you move.

## The Electrodes: How Voltage Gets From Chest to Paper

An ECG electrode is a patch of conductive material (silver, silver-chloride) glued to skin over a thin layer of conductive gel. The electrode doesn't measure the cardiac electrical activity directly — the activity is happening meters away, inside the torso.

Instead, the electrode detects the *voltage gradient* that the depolarization wavefront creates in the tissue around it. This is a capacitive coupling effect: changing electric fields induce voltage across conductors.

The voltage detected is **tiny** — millivolts, microvolt-range in the leads looking perpendicular to the wavefront. The ECG machine amplifies it ~1000×, digitizes it, and prints or displays it.

**What corrupts this signal?** Anything that adds voltage that isn't from the heart:
- **Baseline wander:** Respiration causes electrode-skin impedance to change; the ECG baseline drifts up and down.
- **Muscle artifact:** When the patient tenses a muscle, thousands of motor units firing create a chaotic voltage that swamps the cardiac signal.
- **60 Hz powerline noise:** Electrical fields from the building wiring inductively couple into the patient and the leads. European facilities run 50 Hz; North America 60 Hz.

This noise is not removed by the amplifier — it's *attenuated* by filtering. And filtering is its own beast (covered in LN04).

## Summary

The ECG is a measurement tool, not a diagnostic tool. It measures **electrical activity, not mechanical work**. The **12-lead system** provides 12 simultaneous windows into the same heart from different angles — frontal plane (limb leads) and transverse plane (precordial leads) — allowing you to localize pathology to a specific region. **Single-lead systems** sacrifice spatial information but capture rate and rhythm. Understanding what the ECG *can and cannot see* is the foundation for every interpretation you'll make.
