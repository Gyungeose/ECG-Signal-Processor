## The Art of Not Overdiagnosing

ECGs are **sensitive**. They detect electrical changes that seem abnormal but are often benign. A stressed medical student checking their own ECG might see a finding and think they're dying — when it's a completely normal variant.

**Golden rule:** Not every unusual finding is pathology. Context matters. The patient's age, fitness level, body habitus, and symptoms all determine whether a finding is benign or concerning.

---

## Early Repolarization

**What it looks like:** A **small, distinctive notch** at the junction between the QRS and the ST segment (the J point). It's most visible in leads looking at the inferior and lateral walls (II, III, aVF, V5–V6).

**Physiology:** The normal repolarization process is gradual. But in some young, healthy people, parts of the epicardium repolarize *slightly faster* than others. This creates a brief voltage asymmetry that shows as a notch on the ECG.

**ECG findings:**
- Notch (or slur) at the J point
- Slight ST elevation (1–3 mm) in the same leads
- T waves are typically tall and symmetric

**Clinical significance:** **Completely benign.** No treatment, no follow-up needed. Present in ~5% of young, healthy adults.

**Why it was historically concerning:** Before our understanding improved, early repolarization was sometimes mistaken for **Brugada syndrome** (a rare congenital condition with sudden death risk) or acute MI. Modern cardiologists recognize it as normal.

**Distinguishing features from MI:**
- Early repolarization: J notch is visible, ST elevation <3 mm, T waves are tall and upright.
- Acute MI: No J notch, ST elevation >2 mm, T waves may be peaked early or inverted later.
- Brugada: **Very different** — high take-off of ST segment, coved shape, more anterior.

> [!tip]- Retrieval Cue
> A 20-year-old athlete has an ECG showing a small notch at the J point with 2 mm ST elevation in V4. What is the most likely diagnosis, and why is this not concerning?

## Sinus Arrhythmia

**What it looks like:** RR intervals that vary in a predictable way — speeding up during inspiration, slowing during expiration.

**Physiology:** The vagus nerve (parasympathetic) modulates the SA node. During inspiration, sympathetic tone increases slightly (vagal tone decreases) → SA node fires faster. During expiration, sympathetic withdraws → vagal tone increases → SA node slows. This creates a rhythmic variation in RR interval synchronized with breathing.

**ECG findings:**
- Varying RR intervals (but with a regular pattern)
- CV ~5–10%
- Rate varies typically 5–15 bpm with respiration
- P waves are normal; P-QRS relationships are consistent

**Clinical significance:** **Completely normal.** Very common in young, healthy people and athletes (high vagal tone).

**When to be concerned:**
- If the variation is *arrhythmic* (not synchronized with breathing) → irregular rhythms
- If the variation is *marked* (50+ bpm range) → investigate

> [!example]- Worked Example
> **24-year-old college student, routine exam:** ECG shows RR intervals of 900, 880, 920, 900, 940, 920, 880 ms in a regular pattern. Nurse thinks it's an arrhythmia. Cardiologist asks the patient to breathe normally and observes the pattern: speeds up with inspiration, slows with expiration. Diagnosis: **sinus arrhythmia (normal)**. No workup needed.

## Voltage Criteria and Body Habitus

The **amplitude** of the QRS depends heavily on the patient's body size and chest wall composition.

**Large QRS (high-voltage):**
- Can be normal in tall, muscular people with thick heart muscle
- Can also indicate **left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)** if accompanied by other signs (ST depression in lateral leads, T wave inversion)

**Small QRS (low-voltage):**
- Normal in obese patients (thicker chest wall insulates the signal)
- Normal in patients with emphysema (air-filled lungs between heart and chest wall)
- Can indicate **pericardial effusion** (fluid insulates the heart) if accompanied by other signs

**Distinguishing normal from pathologic:**
- Normal: QRS voltage correlates with body size; no other abnormal findings
- Pathologic: QRS voltage deviation + ST/T wave changes + clinical symptoms

## Poor R-Wave Progression (PRWP)

**What it looks like:** The R wave in the precordial leads doesn't grow larger as expected when moving from V1 → V6. Instead, it stays small or regresses.

**Normal progression:** R wave grows from small in V1 to large in V5–V6 (left ventricle dominates).

**Poor progression:** R wave is small in V1–V2 (normal), should grow in V3, but doesn't — it stays small or even regresses.

**Causes:**

1. **Normal variant:** Some people have naturally small R waves. No pathology.

2. **Anterior infarction:** Dead tissue doesn't depolarize. The R wave fails to appear. Indicates prior (or acute) anterior MI.

3. **COPD:** Emphysema hyperinflates the lungs, pushing the heart posteriorly. Anterior leads are now farther from the ventricles, so the signal is smaller. Not true loss of function — just altered geometry.

4. **Lateral wall MI or other anterior wall pathology.**

**How to distinguish:**
- **Normal variant:** Patient is asymptomatic, no history of chest pain, troponin is negative.
- **Prior MI:** PRWP on an old ECG; current ECG shows Q waves in V1–V3.
- **Acute anterior MI:** PRWP + ST elevation in V1–V4 + elevated troponin.
- **COPD:** Clinical history of emphysema, likely RAD and RVH on the same ECG.

> [!question]- Feynman Check
> A patient with known COPD has poor R-wave progression on ECG. The clinician notes: "This is expected in emphysema." Why does COPD cause PRWP?

## Persistent Juvenile Pattern

Some people retain a pattern typically seen in children: **small or absent R wave in V1–V2, prominent S wave in V1–V2, with ST elevation and upright T wave in V1.**

This looks **dangerously** like **anterior MI** or **Brugada syndrome** on first glance.

**Context saves you:**
- Asymptomatic patient (no chest pain)
- Known to have this pattern for years (unchanged on old ECGs)
- No troponin elevation
- No other signs of MI or Brugada

**Diagnosis:** Normal variant. Usually seen in young, thin people.

## Voltage Variation by Position

Changing the patient's **posture** changes the ECG:

- **Supine (lying flat):** Heart is more horizontal. Some leads see more of the ventricles; amplitudes increase.
- **Upright (sitting):** Heart is more vertical. Amplitudes decrease slightly.
- **Left lateral decubitus:** Shifts cardiac position; can change voltages and axis.

**Clinical reality:** Always record ECGs with the patient supine (if possible) for standardization. If the patient can't lie flat (respiratory distress, etc.), note the position on the ECG. Comparing an upright ECG to an old supine ECG can falsely suggest interval changes in voltage.

## Athlete's Heart

Endurance athletes develop **cardiac remodeling** from years of training. Their ECGs can look abnormal but reflect healthy adaptation:

**Common "abnormal" findings in athletes:**
- **Bradycardia:** HR 40–50 bpm at rest (high vagal tone)
- **Right axis deviation:** Heart position more vertical due to expanded stroke volume
- **Increased QRS voltage:** Mild hypertrophy (left ventricular remodeling)
- **Sinus arrhythmia:** High HRV, very responsive ANS
- **T wave inversion in V1–V2:** Normal in some athletes
- **Incomplete right bundle branch block (IRBBB):** Delayed right ventricular repolarization

**Distinguished from pathology by:**
- Asymptomatic (no syncope, palpitations, dyspnea)
- Excellent exercise tolerance
- Echocardiogram shows normal function (not dilated, not hypertrophied beyond expectations)
- Family history of longevity, not sudden cardiac death

## Long QT in Females

Women naturally have **longer QT intervals than men** (by ~40 ms on average) due to hormonal effects on repolarization.

**Clinical consequence:** A QTc that's "borderline long" in a woman might be normal for her sex, but alarm bells go off if you use a uniform cutoff.

**Better approach:** Use sex-specific normal ranges:
- Men: QTc <440 ms (normal), 440–460 ms (borderline), >460 ms (prolonged)
- Women: QTc <450 ms (normal), 450–470 ms (borderline), >470 ms (prolonged)

This is a recent shift in clinical practice; older textbooks use unified cutoffs.

## High-Takeoff ST Elevation

Some people have **ST elevation in V1–V2** that looks alarming (could be anterior MI or Brugada) but is a **normal variant** called **early repolarization** or **high J waves**.

**Distinguishing from MI:**
- Normal variant: small J wave/notch, ST elevation 1–2 mm, symmetric upright T wave, no reciprocal changes.
- MI: no J notch, ST elevation >2–3 mm, T wave may peak early or invert later, reciprocal ST depression elsewhere.
- Brugada: **coved** ST (ST goes up, then sharply back down, creating a triangular shape), very characteristic in precordial leads.

---

## Summary

Normal variants are ECG findings that *look* abnormal but are benign and require no treatment. **Early repolarization**, **sinus arrhythmia**, **athlete's heart**, and **voltage variation by body habitus** are common. The key to not overdiagnosing is **context**: Is the patient symptomatic? Is this a new finding or an old, stable one? What do other tests show (troponin, echocardiogram, exercise test)? Not every unusual ECG finding is pathology.
