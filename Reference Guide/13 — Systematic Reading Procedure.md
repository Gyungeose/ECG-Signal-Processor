## Why Sequence Matters

Walk into the ED with an acute MI, hand someone an ECG, and ask: "What does this show?" If they read it haphazardly — glancing at the QRS, then checking intervals, then looking for ST elevation — they'll spend two minutes when they have 30 seconds to make a diagnosis.

A **systematic sequence** ensures you don't miss findings. It prioritizes what's most time-critical first. And it's the same every time, so muscle memory takes over and you work faster and more accurately.

---

## The Sequence: Rate → Rhythm → Axis → Intervals → Morphology → Territory

### **1. Rate**

**First question:** How fast is the heart beating?

**How to measure:**
- Count squares between R peaks (300 method) or calculate from RR interval
- Note if rate is normal (60–100), bradycardic (<60), or tachycardic (>100)

**Why first?** Because rate dramatically narrows the differential diagnosis. A regular rhythm at 150 bpm is not sinus tachycardia (which tops out around 140 bpm at rest). It's likely a re-entrant arrhythmia (SVT, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia).

**Immediate clinical context:** Is the rate appropriate for the clinical state? A 120 bpm rate is expected in a septic patient (appropriate tachycardia). A 120 bpm rate in a sleeping, asymptomatic patient is abnormal (inappropriate tachycardia).

### **2. Rhythm**

**Second question:** Is this sinus rhythm or something else?

**Checklist:**
- Are RR intervals regular? (CV < 5% = regular)
- Are there P waves? (And are they normal?)
- Is there a P wave before *every* QRS? (1:1 conduction)
- Is the PR interval constant? (constant AV delay)
- Is the QRS narrow? (<0.12 sec = normal conduction pathway)

**If all checks pass:** Normal sinus rhythm. Move on confidently.

**If any check fails:** Dive deeper.
- No organized P waves + chaotic baseline + irregular RR → likely AFib
- Regular rhythm + P waves at wrong timing + narrow QRS → likely atrial tachycardia or junctional rhythm
- Wide QRS at regular rate → likely ventricular tachycardia (or SVT with aberrancy, but assume VT until proven otherwise in acute setting)

**Why second?** Because rhythm determines urgency. Sinus rhythm, even at an abnormal rate, is typically safer than VT. AFib at RVR (rapid ventricular response) is unstable and needs immediate rate control.

### **3. Axis**

**Third question:** Is the QRS axis normal, or is it deviated?

**Quick method:**
- Lead I positive? Yes = normal or LAD
- Lead aVF positive? 
  - Yes (with I positive) = normal axis. Done.
  - No (with I positive) = LAD.
  - No (with I negative) = RAD.
  - Yes (with I negative) = extreme axis (rare, recheck leads).

**Why third?** Axis gives you clues about the heart's position and any chronic changes (LVH, RVH). It's not immediately life-threatening, so it comes after rate and rhythm. But it's still important for the full picture.

### **4. Intervals**

**Fourth question:** Are the intervals normal, or is something prolonged/shortened?

**Measure:**
- **PR interval:** 0.12–0.20 sec. Prolonged? Shortened?
- **QRS duration:** <0.12 sec. Widened?
- **QT/QTc:** Rate-corrected. Normal ranges?
- **ST segment:** At baseline, elevated, or depressed?

**Clinical significance by interval:**
- Prolonged PR → AV block or slow AV node (medication, disease)
- Shortened PR + delta wave → WPW (re-entry risk)
- Wide QRS → bundle branch block or ventricular rhythm
- Prolonged QTc → torsades de pointes risk; check medications and electrolytes
- ST elevation → acute transmural ischemia (MI)
- ST depression → subendocardial ischemia (ischemia without infarction yet)

**Why fourth?** These findings are important but not immediately life-threatening in the way that rate and rhythm are. They guide management (e.g., avoiding QT-prolonging drugs) but don't demand immediate action like VT does.

### **5. Morphology**

**Fifth question:** Do the waveforms (QRS, T wave, P wave) look normal, or is there unusual shape?

**Assessment:**
- **QRS:** Is it a simple peak, or is it notched? Does it have distinctive shapes suggesting bundle branch block (RBBB = rsR' in V1, LBBB = broad notch in V5)?
- **T wave:** Is it upright where it should be? Any suspicious inversions (could indicate ischemia)?
- **P wave:** Is it normal (upright in II)? Any unusual morphology (could indicate ectopic rhythm or atrial enlargement)?

**Examples:**
- Large, notched QRS in V5 with T wave inversion = LBBB
- Peaked, narrow T waves = hyperkalemia
- Flattened T waves in multiple leads = subendocardial ischemia
- Biphasic P wave in V1 = possible atrial enlargement

**Why fifth?** Morphology is the most detailed analysis. It's information-rich but requires time and careful observation. It's less immediately critical than rate/rhythm/intervals but essential for complete assessment.

### **6. Territory (Localization)**

**Sixth question:** If there's pathology (like ST elevation), which part of the heart is affected?

**Method:**
- Note which leads show ST elevation (or T wave inversion, or Q waves)
- Map those leads to cardiac territories (inferior, anterior, lateral, etc.)
- Determine which coronary artery is likely occluded

**Examples:**
- ST elevation in II, III, aVF → inferior wall → likely RCA
- ST elevation in V1–V4 + I, aVL → anterior wall → likely LAD
- ST elevation in V5–V6 + I, aVL → anterolateral → LAD or LCx

**Why sixth?** This step is critical for MI management (determining which artery to open) but only applies if you've already identified pathology in earlier steps. If the ECG is normal or shows non-specific changes, this step might not apply.

## Integration: Putting It All Together

Once you've worked through all six steps, synthesize:

**Example 1: Acute Anterior STEMI**
1. Rate: 88 (normal)
2. Rhythm: Regular, upright P in II, constant PR → Normal sinus rhythm
3. Axis: Normal
4. Intervals: PR normal, QRS normal width, but **ST elevation >2 mm in V1–V4**
5. Morphology: QRS appears normal, but T waves are peaked early
6. Territory: ST elevation in V1–V4 → **Anterior wall** → **LAD occlusion**

**Clinical action:** Activate catheterization lab. Patient needs urgent PCI.

**Example 2: Chronic Atrial Fibrillation**
1. Rate: 94 (normal, rate-controlled)
2. Rhythm: Irregular (RR intervals vary 600–750 ms), **no P waves**, chaotic baseline → **Atrial fibrillation**
3. Axis: Normal
4. Intervals: QRS narrow, QTc normal, no ST changes
5. Morphology: QRS normal, T waves normal
6. Territory: No pathology

**Clinical action:** Patient already on rate control. Check anticoagulation status. No acute intervention needed.

**Example 3: Acute Inferior STEMI with RV Involvement**
1. Rate: 56 (bradycardic)
2. Rhythm: Regular, P before QRS, constant PR → Normal sinus rhythm
3. Axis: Normal (inferior MI doesn't change axis acutely)
4. Intervals: **ST elevation in II, III, aVF** (inferior), reciprocal ST depression in V1–V2
5. Morphology: Normal QRS
6. Territory: **Inferior wall**, likely RCA occlusion

**Additional step (because inferior MI):** Check V4R (right-sided lead). If ST elevation present → RV infarction.

**Clinical action:** If RV involved, **aggressive fluid resuscitation**. Activate PCI lab. Be careful with nitrates (drop preload too much in RV infarction).

## Comparing to Prior ECGs: The Time-Vector

**Never read an ECG in isolation.** The most valuable information is *change over time*.

**Example:** An ECG shows T-wave inversion in V2–V3. Is this:
- **New?** (suggestive of recent anterior ischemia or infarction)
- **Old?** (prior MI; may have been there for years)
- **Chronic?** (known prior anterior MI)

**Method:**
1. Get a prior ECG from the same patient (from the previous week, month, or year)
2. Place the new and old side by side
3. Ask: "What's different?"

**What to look for:**
- New ST elevation → acute ischemia
- New Q waves → new infarction
- New T-wave inversion → new ischemia
- New bundle branch block → conduction disease
- Axis shift → possible acute event
- Rate change → rate-dependent (may be explained by activity, anxiety, etc.)

**Red flag patterns:**
- **Dynamic ST changes:** ST elevation → T-wave inversion → resolution of T-wave inversion over hours/days. This is the evolution of acute MI.
- **Serial troponin + ECG changes = diagnosis of MI** (requires both biochemical and electrical evidence)

## Clinical Context Shapes Interpretation

The same ECG finding can mean different things depending on the clinical story:

**ST elevation in V1–V2:**
- **If** patient presents with acute chest pain, SOB, diaphoresis: **Anterior STEMI.** Emergent catheterization.
- **If** asymptomatic, healthy 22-year-old: likely **early repolarization** (benign normal variant). Reassure.
- **If** fever, pericardial friction rub: likely **acute pericarditis.** Anti-inflammatories, no emergent catheterization.

**The same ECG. Three different diagnoses.** Context is king.

## Memorization vs. Understanding

There are *hundreds* of ECG patterns. You cannot memorize them all. Instead, understand the **principles:**

1. **Rate reflects autonomic state:** Tachycardia = sympathetic drive; bradycardia = vagal tone or AV block
2. **Rhythm reflects conduction origin:** P waves = atrial; no P waves = AFib; wide QRS = ventricular
3. **Axis reflects heart position and chamber size:** LAD = LVH; RAD = RVH or emphysema
4. **Intervals reflect conduction speed and repolarization time:** Prolonged PR = slow AV node; wide QRS = slow conduction
5. **Morphology reveals shape changes:** Notches, inversions, peaked waves = specific pathology
6. **Territory localizes pathology:** ST elevation in leads tells you which coronary artery

Master these principles, and you can interpret any ECG. The patterns are just applications of the underlying principles.

## The ED ECG: When You Have 30 Seconds

**Triage scenario:** Patient with chest pain. You have 30 seconds to screen the ECG and decide: "Does this patient need the catheterization lab right now?"

**30-second read:**
1. **Rate:** Normal or fast/slow? (Gives a sense of acuity)
2. **Rhythm:** Regular? P waves present? (Distinguish ischemia from arrhythmia)
3. **ST segments:** Elevated? Depressed? (THE critical finding)
4. **Compare to prior:** New changes? (If prior not available, assume new until proven otherwise)

If ST elevation (new) + chest pain + abnormal troponin: **STEMI. Activate catheterization lab.**

Everything else is detail you'll sort out while the patient's on the table.

---

## Summary

A systematic approach ensures you don't miss findings and work efficiently: **Rate → Rhythm → Axis → Intervals → Morphology → Territory**. Each step answers a specific question and has clinical urgency. Prior ECGs are invaluable; changes over time reveal ischemia and infarction. Clinical context shapes interpretation; the same finding means different things in different patients. Understand the principles (what each component represents physiologically), and pattern recognition becomes natural.
