## Why Territory Matters

An MI affecting the inferior wall produces **different ECG changes** than an MI affecting the anterior wall. A bundle branch originating in the **right ventricle** produces different morphology than one from the **left ventricle**. The reason: different parts of the heart are "visible" to different leads.

Understanding which lead looks where is the foundation of **localizing pathology** — answering "Where in the heart is the problem?"

---

## Limb Leads and Frontal Plane Territories

Limb leads divide the frontal plane into 6 regions. Each region corresponds to a cardiac territory:

```
        aVL  |  aVR
    (lateral) | (lateral)
              |
    I (lateral) • aVL (lateral)
              |
    II (inferior) | 
              |
       aVF (inferior)
         III (inferior)
```

### **Inferior Leads: II, III, aVF**

**Anatomical territory:** Inferior wall of the left ventricle and bottom of the right atrium.

**Blood supply:** Typically the **right coronary artery (RCA)**, occasionally the left circumflex (LCx).

**ECG in inferior MI:**
- ST elevation in II, III, aVF (≥1–2 mm)
- Reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL (opposite wall reflection)
- Tall R waves in III (the infarcted tissue doesn't depolarize, so the vector is *pulled away*)

**Clinical pearl:** Inferior MI often involves the RV. Check leads V4R (right-sided lead, placed at 4th intercostal, right sternal border). If there's ST elevation in V4R, the right ventricle is infarcting. This means **do not give IV fluids aggressively** (the RV needs adequate preload, but it's also more sensitive to volume overload). Maintain systolic BP.

### **Lateral Leads: I, aVL, V5–V6**

**Anatomical territory:** Left lateral wall of the left ventricle.

**Blood supply:** Left anterior descending (LAD) or left circumflex (LCx).

**ECG in lateral MI:**
- ST elevation in I, aVL, V5–V6
- Q waves in I, aVL (in older/complete MI)
- Reciprocal changes in III, aVF

### **Anterior Leads: V1–V4**

**Anatomical territory:** Anterior wall, septum, and apex of the left ventricle.

**Blood supply:** **Left anterior descending (LAD)** — the major coronary artery. LAD occlusion is often the most serious.

**Precordial lead breakdown:**
- **V1–V2:** Septum and right ventricle (most right, most anterior)
- **V3:** Anterior wall, apex
- **V4:** Anterior wall, apex

**ECG in anterior MI:**
- ST elevation in V1–V4 (sometimes extending to V5–V6 if extensive)
- Deep Q waves in V1–V3 (old infarct)
- Reciprocal changes in II, III, aVF

**Clinical urgency:** Anterior MI from LAD occlusion is *bad*. The LAD perfuses the apex (where the electrical system is vulnerable) and a large portion of the left ventricle (the main pump). Anterior STEMI has high mortality if not rapidly reperfused.

### **Posterior Leads: V7–V9 (not usually recorded, but important concept)**

**Anatomical territory:** Posterior wall of the left ventricle.

**Blood supply:** Right coronary artery or left circumflex.

**Why not usually recorded?** Posterior MI produces **reciprocal ST depression in V1–V2** (the opposite wall reflection). If you see ST depression in V1–V2 with inferior ST elevation, suspect **posteroinferior MI**.

To confirm posterior MI, place **right-sided leads**: V8 and V9 (same horizontal level as V4, but 5th and 6th intercostal spaces). If these show ST elevation, it's posterior.

## Precordial Leads (V1–V6) and Anterior-Posterior Mapping

The precordial leads are arranged in a line across the chest, progressing from **right to left**:

- **V1:** 4th intercostal space, right sternal border (over the RV, looking at the septum from the right)
- **V2:** 4th intercostal space, left sternal border
- **V3:** Midway between V2 and V4
- **V4:** 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line (over the apex)
- **V5:** 5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line
- **V6:** 5th intercostal space, midaxillary line

**Key principle:** As you move left (V1 → V6), the lead "sees" deeper into the left ventricle and more of its lateral wall.

**Progression of the QRS in a normal heart:**
- **V1:** Small R, large S (the septum depolarizes left-to-right; V1 sees it as a negative wavefront)
- **V2–V3:** Transition zone (R and S are roughly equal)
- **V4–V6:** Large R, small or absent S (the left ventricle dominates; depolarization is moving toward these left-sided leads)

**Poor R-wave progression (PRWP):** If the R wave is too small in V1–V3 (or even absent), it suggests:
- Anteroseptal infarction (the anterior wall isn't depolarizing normally)
- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) — the hyperinflated lungs push the heart posteriorly, so the anterior leads are farther from the depolarizing ventricles
- Normal variant in some people (especially those with emphysema or very tall stature)

> [!tip]- Retrieval Cue
> You see an ECG with a large S wave in V5. In a healthy person, V5 should have a large R and small S. What does a large S in V5 suggest about the cardiac pathology?

## Lead Reversal: A Clinical Trap

Sometimes ECG electrodes are placed in the wrong position or recorded in the wrong order. This produces bizarre abnormalities.

**Example:** Accidentally reversing the left and right arm electrodes results in:
- Lead I is inverted (it's now looking right-to-left instead of left-to-right)
- Axis is extreme or nonsensical
- ST segments look inverted in leads where they should be normal

**Rule:** If you see an ECG with an *extreme* axis, *inverted P waves everywhere*, or findings that make no physiologic sense — **recheck lead placement before diagnosing pathology.**

## Reciprocal Changes

In MI, leads **opposite** to the infarcted zone often show ST *depression* instead of ST elevation. This is the **reciprocal change** — it's not a separate infarction; it's the opposite wall's view of the dead tissue.

**Example:** In **inferior MI** (II, III, aVF show ST elevation), the **anterior leads** (V1–V3) often show ST *depression*. This is reciprocal, not an anterior infarction. It actually indicates a more extensive inferior infarction.

**Clinical pearl:** Reciprocal changes help confirm the diagnosis. They make MI localization easier and more confident.

> [!question]- Feynman Check
> In an acute inferior STEMI, why do leads opposite the infarction (like V1–V2) show ST depression rather than being normal?

## aVR: The "Right Atrium" Lead

**Lead aVR** points from the heart toward the right shoulder — directly opposite lead I. It looks at the *right side* of the heart and the reciprocal direction from the left ventricle.

**Normally:** The QRS in aVR is **inverted** (because the main ventricular depolarization is moving *away* from this lead).

**In acute MI:** aVR can show ischemic changes that help localize the occlusion. Certain patterns (like ST elevation in aVR with diffuse ST depression) suggest **left main coronary occlusion** — a massive, life-threatening blockage.

---

## Concordance and Morphology

**Discordant leads:** Leads looking at opposite sides of the heart often have opposite QRS polarity. This is *normal*.

- If V1 shows negative QRS (small R, large S), V5 should show positive QRS (large R, small S).

**Concordant leads (unusual):** If V1 and V5 both show QRS pointing the same direction (both positive or both negative), something abnormal is happening:
- Severe hyperkalemia
- Ventricular rhythm with abnormal origin
- Severe right or left ventricular hypertrophy
- Sometimes normal in certain body habitus

---

## Summary

Each lead sees a specific anatomical territory. **Limb leads** (I, II, III, aVF) localize in the frontal plane: lateral, inferior, anterior regions. **Precordial leads** (V1–V6) progressively deepen from right to left, revealing septal, anterior, and lateral walls. **Reciprocal changes** — ST changes opposite to the infarcted zone — confirm MI and indicate extensiveness. Understanding lead territories transforms the ECG from a waveform into a **roadmap of cardiac pathology**.
